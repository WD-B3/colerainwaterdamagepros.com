Barton is an unincorporated community in southern Colerain Township, Belmont County, Ohio. It sits along Wheeling Creek and uses ZIP code 43905. A shared ZIP code is not a city limit, so Barton remains part of Colerain Township and is not a separate town.

This page is for people who live in or near Barton and need water removed, a basement dried or a leak repaired. Call (740) 231-9132, say you are in Barton and describe what is wet. The dispatcher will ask about the source, the depth and whether power to the area is still on.

Where Barton sits and how it began

Public references say the Barton post office has operated since 1880 and that the community was platted in 1905. It was named for Abner Barton, the original owner of the land, and it began as a mining community.

Colerain Township trustees have taken part in county meetings about Barton and the creek, which shows the community is handled as part of the township. The reports we found describe particular sections and events. They do not give a count of Barton homes with basements, so this page makes no claim about housing types.

Wheeling Creek and the flood record

An August 2015 Times Leader editorial said Wheeling Creek overflows its banks too often in Barton. It named major floods in 1995 and 2004 and said two people died in the Barton area. It reported that the East Loretta section takes the worst of the water.

The same editorial said about 40 houses and 120 people are affected when the creek floods, and it described water in basements and yards as a health hazard. It also said dredging was proposed, which would need county permission and Army Corps permitting. That is an editorial position, and we have not confirmed that any work was completed.

The history is older than that. A published county history describes the storm of August 12, 1866, when Wheeling Creek left its banks, and it names Barton as a landmark in that account.

More recently, the Times Leader reported that Church Street in Barton flooded during the June 2025 flash flooding around Wheeling. The paper said flooding was less severe in Belmont County than across the river in Ohio County, West Virginia.

None of this means every Barton home is at risk. Many homes may never see creek water, and a failed supply line, a leaking water heater or a clogged drain can damage any house in any ZIP code.

Creek water is not clean water

Water from a creek has crossed roads, yards and soil before it reaches a building, so treat it as contaminated. Keep children and pets out of it and do not wade through it. Household fans and ordinary vacuums are not enough for contaminated water, and they can spread it.

If water has reached outlets, appliances or the panel, leave the room. Call the utility or an electrician before touching anything. Do not operate breakers while standing in water.

Insurance and flood coverage

Homeowners policies usually exclude water that rises from a creek, according to the Ohio Department of Insurance, and flood coverage is a separate policy. Coverage depends on the cause of the damage and the terms of your policy, so ask your insurer before you decide anything.

Photos, a list of damaged items and the time you first saw the water give you a record to share with your insurer. We do not decide coverage, and we cannot promise what an insurer will pay.

Flood maps and permits in Belmont County

Barton is in Belmont County, where a Flood Hazard Area Development Permit applies to building work in mapped flood hazard areas. The county floodplain administrator works through the Belmont County Commissioners in Saint Clairsville.

Standards behind this page

Under ANSI/IICRC S500, water that comes from a creek or river is Category 3, which means it may carry contamination. That category decides what is removed, what is dried and what is discarded, so the first assessment matters more than speed alone.

Safety steps for creek water

  • Stay out of standing creek water, and keep children and pets away from it.
  • Do not touch wet outlets, cords or appliances.
  • Do not flip breakers while standing in water. Call the utility or an electrician.
  • Do not use household fans or an ordinary vacuum on contaminated water.
  • Wear boots and gloves if you must enter a cleared area, and wash well afterward.
