About

Who we are

Colerain Water Damage Pros is a dispatch service for water damage in Colerain Township, Belmont County, Ohio, and the towns around it. One phone number reaches the line around the clock, and crews are sent for water removal, structural drying and repair.

The township is in the northeastern part of Belmont County and was organized in 1808. It is named after Coleraine in Northern Ireland, and it is not the Colerain Township near Cincinnati.

How a job runs

You call, describe the water and the source, and the dispatcher explains the first steps. Crews take meter readings, explain the scope and the pricing method and start work once you agree. Drying is tracked with readings until the structure reaches a dry standard.

  1. Call (740) 231-9132, any hour.
  2. Stop the source if you can do so safely.
  3. A crew assesses the damage with meters.
  4. You hear the scope and the price method before work starts.
  5. Water comes out, the structure dries and repairs follow.

What we do and what we do not

We handle water damage, from burst pipes and floods to wet floors and walls. We do not offer mold remediation, we do not install radon systems and we do not repair plumbing, roofs or equipment. When the source needs another trade, we say so.

Our address is 51710 National Rd E, Saint Clairsville, OH 43950. Calls are the fastest way to reach a crew. Calls to our line may be recorded for quality and training purposes.

Why local conditions shape the work

Water damage here follows the land. Wheeling Creek runs through Blaine and Lansing, both partly inside the township, and runoff can rise quickly after hard rain. Belmont County's worst documented flood, on Pipe Creek and Wegee Creek in June 1990, came from violent thunderstorms, and the county again reported heavy flood damage in April 2024.

Housing stock matters too. Many homes in the river towns date from the early twentieth century and have plaster walls, stone or block basements and plumbing that has been patched many times. Hilltop homes near Saint Clairsville and Mount Pleasant face more roof, runoff and supply line problems than river flooding.

That is why the crews take readings before deciding what to remove. Plaster, hardwood and block walls dry slowly, and a plan built for a new drywall house does not fit them.

What to do before you call

Before you call, it helps to know a few things. Where is the water coming from, and can you reach the shutoff? Is anyone at risk from wet electrical equipment? Do you know your ZIP code and the age of the house?

  • Stop the water if you can do so safely.
  • Do not touch wet outlets, cords or appliances.
  • Photograph the damage before you move things.
  • Keep children and pets out of the wet area.

The standard behind the work

The industry standard for professional water damage work is ANSI/IICRC S500. It describes water categories, drying classes and monitoring, and it is the language insurers and restoration companies use when they describe a loss. Ask any company you are thinking of hiring how it uses the standard on a real job.

A good answer mentions meter readings, a dry standard for each material, daily monitoring and a written record. A weak answer mentions only equipment and days.

How we think about the job

Honest scope. Dispatch tells you what the crew can do and what needs another trade. A plumber stops and repairs a leaking line, a roofer repairs a roof and a waterproofing contractor changes drainage so water stays out. We dry and restore what the water damaged.

Honest pricing. Pricing is explained after the assessment and before work starts, and the method is written down so you can share it with your insurer. We do not quote a number over the phone because no one can know the size of the job until it is measured.

Honest limits. We do not promise an arrival time, because roads, weather and the number of active jobs change daily. Same-day response is offered when available, and the dispatcher gives you the best estimate they have when you call.

What to expect when you call

A call to the line goes to a dispatcher, not a recording tree. Have your address and ZIP ready, say what is wet and say where the water is coming from if you know. If you cannot reach the shutoff, say so, because the dispatcher can often talk you to it.

After the call, a crew is sent and you hear the next step. If you are renting, tell the dispatcher who the landlord is. If your insurer wants a claim number or a record of the call, tell the crew. Everything is written down so the paperwork does not become a second emergency.

Questions are welcome. If you are not sure whether the damage is covered by your policy, whether a leak is serious or whether a repair needs another trade, ask. A clear answer on the first call saves a second one.
