Water Damage Categories 1, 2 and 3 in Colerain, OH

By the Colerain Water Damage Pros team

The category of the water decides what can be saved, what must be thrown away and how the crew protects itself.

When a restoration company asks what the water looked like or where it came from, it is sorting the loss into a category. The category is one of the most important facts in any water damage job, because it determines what happens next. This guide explains the three categories in plain terms.

The categories come from the ANSI/IICRC S500 standard for professional water damage restoration, which describes them by the source and the level of contamination.

The three categories

CategoryCommon nameTypical sources
Category 1.Clean water.Broken supply line, tub overflow with no contaminants, melted ice.
Category 2.Gray water.Washer or dishwasher discharge, sump pump overflow, toilet overflow with urine but no feces.
Category 3.Black water.Sewage, river or creek flooding, standing water that has sat and grown bacteria.

These labels describe the water at the source, and they can change.

Category 1 in detail

Clean water does not pose a substantial health risk at the moment it is released. A pipe that bursts, a water heater that fails or a faucet left running all produce Category 1 water at first. Porous materials such as carpet and drywall can often be dried and saved if the crew acts quickly.

Category 2 in detail

Gray water has some contamination that could cause illness if swallowed or if it touches broken skin. A washing machine discharge, a dishwasher backup or water from a sump pump are typical. Porous materials are more likely to be removed than in Category 1, and surfaces are cleaned and disinfected.

Category 3 in detail

Black water is grossly contaminated. Sewage backups, rising creek or river water and long-standing water all qualify. It can carry bacteria, viruses and chemicals. Porous materials that touched it, such as carpet, pad, insulation and drywall, are usually discarded. Crews wear protective gear, and hard surfaces are cleaned and disinfected.

The sewage backup page and the flood damage page explain the handling in more detail.

How categories get worse with time

Clean water that sits for a day or two in a warm building can degrade. Bacteria grow in wet materials and in standing water, and what began as Category 1 can become Category 2 or 3. That is one more reason to act fast. Clean-water losses that are dried quickly stay clean-water losses.

What this means for your belongings

CategoryUsual treatment of porous items
1.Dry and save when possible.
2.Clean and dry or replace, depending on the item.
3.Discard porous items. Clean and disinfect hard items.

For furniture decisions, the same logic applies. Wet upholstery from dirty water is usually a loss.

Category 3 is common after Belmont County floods

Creek and river floods are Category 3 events. The 1990 flood in the Pipe Creek and Wegee Creek basins and the April 2024 floods in Belmont County both sent muddy, contaminated water into buildings. Homes near the creek mouths and river towns, such as Bridgeport and Martins Ferry, should treat any flood water as Category 3. A leaking pipe in a hilltop home is a different story.

Sources: ANSI/IICRC S500 category descriptions, USGS, Belmont County EMA.

Safety before the crew arrives

If you suspect Category 2 or 3 water, stay out of it, keep children and pets away and wash your hands with soap and clean water after any contact. Do not use household vacuums, and do not run the HVAC if sewage may be in the ducts. Describe the source to the dispatcher so the crew arrives prepared.

Why categories matter for price

Higher categories take more protective gear, more removal and more disposal, so jobs cost more. Telling the crew the true source up front avoids surprises. The water extraction page explains the first steps for all three.

How crews protect themselves

For Category 2 and 3 water, crews wear gloves, boots, eye protection and respirators, and they contain the work area so contamination does not spread to clean rooms. Equipment is cleaned between jobs. Ask the crew about their protective gear if the water was dirty. A company that skips it is not following the standard.

Hard surfaces such as concrete and tile are cleaned with detergent and treated with a disinfectant. Porous materials are bagged and removed. The crew then dries the structure and checks the readings.

A few real-world examples

A supply line that bursts in a kitchen is Category 1 at first. A washer that overflows with soapy water is Category 2. A toilet that backs up from the main line is Category 3, and so is creek water that rises into a basement. A flooded basement after a storm may have started as rain but picked up soil, oil and debris, so it is handled as contaminated.

If you are unsure, say so when you call. The dispatcher will ask about the source, odor and color, and the crew will make the call on site.

FAQ

Questions about water categories

Can Category 1 water become dangerous?

Yes. If it sits for a day or more, bacteria can grow and the category can rise.

Is rainwater Category 1?

Rain through a roof starts relatively clean, but it picks up contaminants in insulation and building materials, so it may be treated as Category 2.

Is toilet overflow always Category 3?

Not always. An overflow with no feces can be Category 2. Sewage from a backed-up line is Category 3.

Who decides the category?

The restoration crew, using the source, the visible condition and the standard. You can describe what you saw to help.

Does the category change what my insurance pays?

It can. Backup and flood are often treated differently from sudden pipe breaks. Ask your insurer.
